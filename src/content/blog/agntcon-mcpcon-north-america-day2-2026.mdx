*Friday morning in the keynote hall: MCP tool calls in Claude, 16x since April.*

Day two of AGNTCon + MCPCon North America, on Friday 23 October 2026, began at breakfast with Tim O'Reilly, then a morning of keynotes and thirteen more interviews, from 09:37 to 16:36, all recorded for [Udienza](/blog/udienza-video-podcast-launch-2026/). The day hour by hour is on udienza.com: [AGNTCon + MCPCon North America 2026: day two](https://www.udienza.com/articles/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-2026-day-two). [My day one post](/blog/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-2026/) covered MCP Tasks, Skills over MCP and elicitation.

Here is what I would pass on to a team that builds or runs agents.

## Breakfast with Tim O'Reilly: MCP and the architecture of participation

Tim O'Reilly, founder and CEO of O'Reilly Media, sat down with me before the keynotes. O'Reilly is an online learning platform today, and its new product is the O'Reilly Expert MCP, in beta with a limited number of corporate customers and rolling out more widely soon: an MCP server that lets your agent call on the experts in its catalogue.

Why experts, when we were told the models would make them unnecessary? Because, in his experience, an LLM has so much information that you have to focus it, the way you capture the power of the sun. He invokes the books like spells: "by the wisdom of Kent Beck or by the wisdom of the Google SRE handbook". His CTO built a research agent that reads the organisation's sources of truth, the product board, Jira and GitHub, and asked it whether a team needed another engineer. The first answer was yes, hire. With the O'Reilly MCP connected, it found the SRE handbook's idea of operational toil, saw how much of it the team carried, and said to fix that first: "it came up with a completely different answer when it was given the focusing power of a particular piece of human expertise". The same goes for tokens: an AI that brute-forces its way to an answer a person already knows burns a lot of them on the way.

Then the bigger point, which he was taking to his keynote: mechanism design, "a kind of reverse game theory". Instead of the optimal strategy for a game's rules, you ask which rules would produce the outcome you want. The game we were told we are playing in AI is a winner-take-all race to superintelligence. With MCP, he said, Anthropic did something unintentional, the way IBM did when it published the specs of the PC: it expected drivers for peripherals and got whole PCs, a clone market and a far bigger market. "MCP changed the market structure of AI", from calling the APIs of the winners to something more networked, collaborative and human-centred.

He tied it to the history he lived. In 1998 he organised the Freeware Summit, where the name open source came from. What made Unix work was not its licence but its design, a small kernel and small utilities that read standard in and write standard out, so anyone could add a piece that fit; "the architecture of participation is the heart of open source", and MCP brings that world back. Training a large model takes massive capital, so the heart of open source is going up the stack, to MCP, skills and agents, where people can actually take part. His last word was on openness: "our willingness to share and cooperate is the human superpower".

## Smaller models, and a loop that never stops

The PyTorch Foundation's keynote came from Jonathan Bryce of the Linux Foundation, and its title, "From Bigger Models to Better Systems", was the argument: on the open LLM leaderboard, models under 13B parameters keep getting more capable at the same size, and small models frequently outperform larger ones.

The example that stayed with me came from Shopify. A small specialist model for one task (buyer profiles) runs in a loop in production: a calibrated judge detects the weak outputs, critics diagnose them and the model replays with their hints, and the accepted repairs become new training data. On the judge's score it went from 75.3 to 78.1 to 84.6 in a week, past the frontier model's 83.0.

![A slide titled A small specialist model can outperform a frontier model, the specialist's score rising from 75.3 to 84.6 above a frontier model line at 83.0](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-specialist-model.jpg)

The lesson for platform teams is not "use small models". It is that the evaluation loop (judge, repair, retrain) is the product, and the model is what it produces. If your agents have no judge in production, that is the piece to build first.

The tools are open, in a PyTorch Foundation that now has six projects: PyTorch, vLLM, DeepSpeed, Ray, Helion and Safetensors.

![A slide listing the six PyTorch Foundation projects: PyTorch, vLLM, DeepSpeed, Ray, Helion and Safetensors](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-pytorch-projects.jpg)

I sat down with Jonathan after his keynote for a 14-minute conversation on PyTorch, vLLM and the foundation, now in production for Udienza.

## Universal properties: guarantees for unpredictable agents

Shadaj Laddad of AWS had the most engineering-minded talk of the conference. His answer to agents writing code you cannot fully predict is the **universal property**: a specification that applies to any application, whatever its logic. Two everyday examples: Rust's borrow checker guarantees no concurrent access to mutable state, and React guarantees that an incremental render is the same as rendering from scratch.

![The full keynote hall in San Jose, the universal properties slide on the side screen](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-universal-properties.jpg)

His two projects apply the idea: Hydro, a framework for correct, agent-written distributed systems, and Infinity, an agent runtime where concurrent agents behave the same as sequential execution. What I took home is his three steps: ask which properties hold for all your applications, formalize them apart from any single app, then enforce them with frameworks, strong types and crafted APIs. An agent can then write the logic, and the property still holds.

## The swap drill

The question I would put to every team that runs agents came from Rao Surapaneni of Google Cloud (VP, Gemini Enterprise & Agent Platform Governance): **can you run a swap drill in under two days?** Swap the model, across frontier, open-weight and proprietary. Swap the harness without rebuilding governance. Swap an agent, through A2A and a registry. And through every swap, identity, policy, observability and your business IP stay intact.

His six best practices read like a checklist for a platform team:

![A slide titled Best Practices with six numbered items for shipping autonomous agents safely](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-best-practices.jpg)

1. Demand standard interfaces in RFPs.
2. Emit OpenTelemetry GenAI traces.
3. Use federated, standards-based identity.
4. Store agent definitions as code.
5. Use an agent gateway to enforce enterprise policies.
6. Evals for everything.

If you run Kubernetes, most of this will sound familiar: declarative definitions in Git, a gateway for policy, standard telemetry. Agents are getting the same platform treatment, and the teams that already have it are ahead.

## MCP in Claude: 16x since April

The biggest number of the day was Kelly Fang's, from Anthropic: **16x** more weekly MCP tool calls through its remote proxy than at the New York MCP Dev Summit in April 2026, and more than 6,000 servers in the Claude directory. One server reaches Claude on the web, desktop and mobile, Claude Code, Claude Managed Agents and Claude in Slack.

![A slide titled MCP is growing fast in Claude: 16x growth in weekly MCP tool calls since April 2026](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-mcp-16x.jpg)

Three builders put numbers on it from their side: Atlassian's MCP at more than 15 million daily tool calls, monday.com's Claude connector at 85,000 accounts, and Linear's issues created through MCP up 30x, more than all its other channels combined. For anyone still deciding whether to ship an MCP server for their product, that slide is the answer.

## On the floor: governance, six ways

The afternoon was interviews, about one every half hour. Listen to them side by side, as I did, and most of the vendors are selling a piece of the same thing: the controls around an agent in production. Each draws the line somewhere else.

- **Airia** puts the controls in a gateway: an MCP gateway and "agent constraints" that block what an agent is not meant to do, managed by IT in one place instead of a settings file on every laptop.
- **Alterion** watches from the network and the endpoint, where many agents have no control plane at all, and classifies their behaviour with its own AI.
- **Celigo** governs the MCP servers its integration platform publishes, down to single tools, and calls the path to autonomy "earned".
- **Diagrid** makes agents durable, so a crash does not send the same email twice, and signs every step an agent takes.
- **AgentCat** looks for the silent failures: an agent that gives up, abandons the session or calls the wrong tool, which logs and traces never show, while the user has a really bad experience.
- **Quesma** collects the agents' session logs, to see what agentic coding is worth and what it costs.

![Luca Berton and Spencer Reagan giving a thumbs-up at the Airia booth](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-airia.jpg)

Then there are the layers underneath: **Mezmo's** AURA, an open source SRE agent with a strict human in the loop before any remediation; **YugabyteDB's** Meko, a context layer of memory and knowledge for every agent; **Safe Software's** FME, which publishes no-code MCP servers over hard-to-reach data like 3D building models and sensor feeds.

![Andre Elizondo and Luca Berton in front of the Mezmo booth wall that reads Fortune tellers make bad SREs](/blog/events/agntcon-mcpcon-north-america-day2-2026/agntcon-na-2026-day2-mezmo.jpg)

**My take:** the questions to ask any of them are the same ones as in Amsterdam. Where does it run (on-premises, air-gapped, on Kubernetes)? Which identity standards does it speak? Does it emit standard telemetry? Can you leave without rewriting every agent? Those are Rao Surapaneni's swap drill, asked of a vendor.
