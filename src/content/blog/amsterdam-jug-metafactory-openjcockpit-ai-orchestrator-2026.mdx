## Metafactory and OpenJCockpit

Metafactory is a Java company. It does consultancy for clients such as banks and government organisations, and it has a product: **OpenJCockpit**, "the open-source AI Software Factory." It started as an AI orchestrator and, as the title says, became much more. The focus is Java code generation with AI, with two things the team cares about most: **control over the process**, so that the result is enterprise-grade software, and **the requirements**, because generating the code is now the easy part.

![Ricky van Rijn presenting his bio slide, 'CTO at MetaFactory', next to the OpenJCockpit banner.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-openjcockpit-ai-orchestrator-2026/jug-metafactory-ricky-bio.jpg)

Ricky has spent more than ten years in engineering, from Java developer to CTO, including solution architecture roles for government. That explains his angle: in enterprises and government, software must pass **audits** and the same requirements must give **reproducible** results, again and again.

## You are the pilot, AI is your crew, quality is the destination

The whole talk is wrapped in an aviation metaphor, and the roll-up banner shows it best. It is a **boarding pass**: *from* **YOU** (the pilot) *to* **QA** (quality), with **you** as pilot, **AI** as crew and **quality** as destination. At the bottom: *"You are the pilot. AI is your crew. Quality is the destination."*

![Title slide 'We created our own AI orchestrator and it became so much more!' with the OpenJCockpit logo, shown while Ricky van Rijn gestures to the audience.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-openjcockpit-ai-orchestrator-2026/jug-metafactory-title-slide.jpg)

The route does not go from *you* to *code*. It goes from you to **QA**. The human is not a typist any more, but the human is still the one who is accountable for where the plane lands.

## Three phases, and where most companies are stuck

![Ricky van Rijn points at the 'Defining phases' slide: Traditional, Assisted, Agentic.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-openjcockpit-ai-orchestrator-2026/jug-metafactory-defining-phases.jpg)

| Phase | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Software engineers write code, in Scrum teams with business analysts, QA, ops and a product owner |
| **Assisted** | AI assists: you prompt, it suggests, Copilot proposes code in your editor, you accept or reject |
| **Agentic** | AI writes code: a team of AI agents with skills, policies and access to MCP servers |

Ricky's view: **most companies are still in the assisted phase**. The agentic phase is the hardest one, because the real question is how much of your work you hand over to agents, *"because you're still accountable for what it produces."* Metafactory also started in the assisted phase, to get a feel for what the AI produces and where to give feedback, before moving to agents.

![Ricky van Rijn presents the slide 'Step by step towards Agentic AI for client projects', with 'AI drafts code' and 'Agents work in parallel' illustrations.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-openjcockpit-ai-orchestrator-2026/jug-metafactory-agentic-step-by-step.jpg)

The slide *"Step by step towards Agentic AI for client projects"* has two halves: **the opportunity** is that AI drafts code, **the multiplier** is that agents work in parallel. Parallel agents bring new problems, for example timing: the output of a business-analyst agent may be needed by the backend and frontend agents at the same moment.

## The use case: from Node.js to Spring Boot

The team needed a real project to try it on: a **Node.js backend to be rebuilt in Java**. Because of their experience, they chose **Spring Boot**. The plan was to use AI not only for the code, but also to write the implementation specs, and to put guardrails and skills around it.

An early lesson was about **objective feedback**. They asked the AI to follow the **ISO/IEC 25010** software quality model. Later they tested it: they showed it a `main` method with everything in it, written in the same session, and the AI said it was fine, because it had generated it. The fix: review in a **new session**, so the reviewer is not judging its own work.

## Agents for every role in the team

To build agents around the development process, they copied the roles of a traditional agile team: business analyst, backend developer, frontend developer and so on. Each **agent definition** describes what the agent does, how it does it, and its borders. (Ricky's aside: you can tell a backend agent not to touch the frontend, and some day it still will.)

The definitions lived on a Confluence page. That worked for a day, maybe a week. After a few weeks, everybody had their own version of each agent. The other problem was **visibility**: which work is available, which agent is active, on which machine, on which task? Everything ran on the developers' own laptops, not in a shared place.

## Specs: from a Jira ticket to an executable contract

The next step was a **Jira MCP server**, so the AI could read the user stories and help turn them into **spec files**. In their definition, a spec file is a structured, version-controlled document, usually Markdown, that is the authoritative source of truth and the executable contract. It should be **functionally reproducible**: not the same code every run, but the same behaviour.

To make specs explicit, they let **the AI interview the human**: where the AI cannot answer something, it asks, and when the human cannot answer either, that becomes a discussion. Each story becomes an executable task with **business context** and **scenarios**, and the scenarios drive the tests: written in Gherkin, they map almost one-to-one to Cucumber tests.

## The first output showed the problems in the input

The first results were instructive, because they exposed what the input did not say:

- **Assumptions.** The AI knows nothing about your domain: the abbreviations, the internal names, the duplicated and outdated Confluence pages every company has.
- **Wrong names, silent decisions.** A story asked for a change "on the dashboard page." The page was called the *start page*, so the AI could not find a dashboard page and **created a new one**, with a lot of new code. It asked nothing.
- **Invented services.** Where the spec described services without more detail, the AI generated the backend services itself.

The response: **smaller spec files**. A large spec always leaves things out, because you know them, or you think the AI should know them. Slice specs the way Scrum teams slice user stories.

The other driver was **review pressure**. One of the first changes came back as a pull request with about **19,525 lines of code changed**. Nobody can review that. Worse, while delivering a feature, the AI had also changed one of his skill files. Ricky's reaction: *"I'm not asking to change my skill. I'm providing you my skill."* The point, he said, is to keep **"the grip and control on your AI and your AI execution."**

## When everybody has their own agents

The less visible effect was on the team. With everyone tuning their own agents, the definitions drifted apart: one developer's agents focused on clean code, another's on secure code. Code reviews turned into clashes between agent definitions, every rule ended up in one big pile of definitions, and the same mistakes kept coming back. The atmosphere, in Ricky's words, became "a little bit rotten." The lesson he shared: **have the discussion** about what good code means, as a team, instead of encoding your own opinion in your own agent.

Decentralised agents also bring technical risks:

- **Different skills and MCP configurations on every machine**, including different MCP servers, which is a security question: what can each agent read and change?
- **Sub-agents talking to each other** for feedback can end up in an almost endless loop. With paid frontier models, you pay for every round, and the result does not get better.
- **"It works on my machine"** is back, now for agents.

## Centralise: one foundation, one source of truth

So they centralised. They built a shared technical foundation on **[Embabel](https://github.com/embabel/embabel-agent)**, the JVM agent framework from Spring creator Rod Johnson, and put the agent definitions, skills and spec files in **one shared, version-controlled place**. The team started developing as a team again, and the tool they built for themselves grew into **OpenJCockpit**.

According to the meetup abstract, OpenJCockpit adds a **visual dashboard** for active agents, tasks and next steps (the answer to the visibility problem above), and it changes the roles: developers review AI output, analysts define requirements, and product owners manage a faster sprint queue.

## My takeaways

This was one of the most honest talks about agentic development I have heard this year, because it was about what went wrong:

1. **The bottleneck moved to the requirements.** Vague stories produce confident, wrong code. Small specs, explicit context and an AI that asks questions are worth more than a better model.
2. **Review in a fresh context.** An agent reviewing its own output in the same session is not a review.
3. **Agent definitions are team infrastructure.** Treat them like code: shared, versioned, reviewed. Personal agents create personal standards.
4. **Size the work for human review.** A 19,525-line pull request is a process failure, not a productivity win.

And the boarding pass still says it best: **the destination is quality, and somebody has to be the pilot.**
