The closing talk at the Amsterdam JUG meetup at **Metafactory** was *"Securing Java for the Quantum Era"* by **Ana-Maria Mihalceanu**, Senior Java Developer Advocate at Oracle. (The earlier talks: OpenJCockpit, an in-house AI orchestrator and Capability Mesh.) She published the deck, so you can follow along: [Securing Java for the Quantum Era (PDF)](https://github.com/ammbra/ammbra/blob/main/Securing%20Java%20for%20the%20Quantum%20Era.pdf).

![Ana-Maria Mihalceanu at the standing desk next to a screen showing the Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap, 2026-11-05 update.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-ana-maria-roadmap.jpg)

She opened with three goals: **understand the urgency** of advancing quantum-safe cryptography, **explore solutions and standards**, and **use JDK security features to build resilient applications**. What I like about the talk is that it ends with **dates and version numbers**. Post-quantum cryptography is usually presented as "something for later." The slides said otherwise: parts of it are already in your JDK update stream.

## Why today's crypto is safe, and why that will change

Public-key cryptography relies on maths that is **easy to compute and not feasible to reverse** on classical computers. The slide uses RSA as the example:

- Multiplying two huge primes is easy. Factoring the resulting semi-prime is impractical.
- An RSA-2048 number is about **617 decimal digits** long, and each hidden prime has roughly **309 digits**.
- Guessing the right factor is on the order of **1 in 10<sup>300</sup>**.
- The largest public RSA factoring record is still far smaller: **RSA-896, a 270-digit semi-prime, factored in 2026** (as stated on the slide).

Then comes the twist.

## Harvest now, decrypt later

An attacker can **record encrypted traffic today** and decrypt it **later**, when the maths stops being a barrier. If your data must stay secret for ten years (health, legal, financial, government), the threat is already here, even if no quantum computer breaks anything today. This is why the talk is titled around *urgency*, not around a future date.

## What a quantum computer breaks, and what it only weakens

The deck separates two algorithms, and this is the slide I would show to a non-specialist manager:

| Quantum algorithm | What it does | Effect | Affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shor's** | Efficiently solves integer factorisation and discrete logarithms | **Effectively breaks** modern asymmetric cryptography | RSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECDH, DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA |
| **Grover's** | Accelerates unstructured search, including brute-force key search | **Weakens** symmetric crypto and hashing, but does not break it outright | AES, SHA-2, SHA-3 |

So the urgent work is on **key exchange and signatures**. Symmetric encryption and hashes need larger parameters, not a replacement.

## Governments are setting deadlines

The slide's message: *"Post-quantum cryptography is no longer just a research objective."*

| Location | Mandate / roadmap | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| United States | Executive Order 14412 (Advanced Cryptographic Attacks) | High-risk systems by **2030-2031**; broader federal migration by **2035** |
| European Union | EU PQC Migration Roadmap | Critical infrastructure by **2030** |
| United Kingdom | NCSC PQC Migration Timeline | Complete migration by **2035** |
| Australia | ASD Information Security Manual | Complete PQC transition by the end of **2030** |

For European companies, the line to remember is **critical infrastructure by 2030**. That is a migration of long-lived systems, and it starts with knowing what you run.

## The standards: HPKE and the NIST FIPS family

Ana-Maria then moved to the building blocks. First **Hybrid Public Key Encryption (HPKE)**, which she summarised as **HPKE = KDF + KEM + AEAD**, quoting RFC 9180: *"HPKE works for any combination of an asymmetric KEM, key derivation function (KDF), and authenticated encryption with additional data (AEAD) encryption function."* In plain words: a key encapsulation step, a key derivation step and authenticated symmetric encryption, combined so you can encrypt to someone's public key safely, with each piece replaceable.

Then the three NIST standards, finalised in August 2024:

| Standard | What it is | Based on |
|---|---|---|
| **FIPS 203** | General encryption (key encapsulation) | **ML-KEM** |
| **FIPS 204** | The primary standard for digital signatures | **ML-DSA** |
| **FIPS 205** | Stateless hash-based digital signature standard | (hash-based signatures) |

## How the JDK got here

The "Advancing JDK Security" slide is a timeline of the JEPs that make Java ready:

| JDK | JEP | What |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | **JEP 452** | Key Encapsulation Mechanism API |
| 24 | **JEP 478** | Key Derivation Function API (Preview) |
| 24 | **JEP 496** | Quantum-Resistant Module-Lattice-Based Key Encapsulation Mechanism (ML-KEM) |
| 24 | **JEP 497** | Quantum-Resistant Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (ML-DSA) |
| 25 | **JEP 510** | Key Derivation Function API |
| 27 | **JEP 527** | Post-Quantum Hybrid Key Exchange for TLS 1.3 |

Read it as a build-up: first the **KEM API** (21), then the **algorithms** (24), the final **KDF API** (25), and finally **hybrid post-quantum key exchange in TLS 1.3** (27). *Hybrid* means the handshake combines a classical algorithm with a post-quantum one, so an attacker has to break both.

## The part that matters most: Oracle's roadmap for the LTS releases

Upstream features are nice, but most enterprises run **LTS releases**, often older ones. The last slides showed Oracle's *JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap* (2026-11-05 update), published at [java.com/en/jre-jdk-cryptoroadmap.html](https://www.java.com/en/jre-jdk-cryptoroadmap.html). I photographed the tables. Here they are as text.

### Already released (2026-10-20)

![Slide 15: Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap, released changes table with ML-KEM, ML-DSA, post-quantum hybrid key exchange for TLS 1.3 and PBMAC1.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-crypto-roadmap-released.jpg)

| Release | Releases affected | Algorithm | What was added |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-10-20 | **25.0.5+9** | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange for **TLS 1.3** |
| 2026-10-20 | **25.0.5+9** | PBMAC1 | PKCS12 KeyStore support for PBMAC1 |
| 2026-10-20 | **21.0.13+10**, **17.0.21+9** | ML-KEM | Key Encapsulation Mechanism |
| 2026-10-20 | **21.0.13+10**, **17.0.21+9** | ML-DSA | Digital Signature Algorithm |

Compare this with the JEP timeline above: ML-KEM and ML-DSA arrived in upstream JDK 24, and Oracle has now **backported them to the older LTS lines** 21 and 17. Hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange (JEP 527, listed under JDK 27) is already in the **25.0.5** update. In the ML-KEM and ML-DSA rows, the change log shows the target date moved from "2026-H2" to a fixed 2026-10-20 on 2026-07-03.

### Planned (target 2027-01-19)

![Slide 14: Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap, planned changes table: ML-DSA signed JARs for 25, 21, 17 and post-quantum hybrid key exchange for TLS 1.3 for 21 and 17.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-crypto-roadmap-planned.jpg)

| Target date | Releases | Algorithm | Planned action |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027-01-19 | 25, 21, 17 | ML-DSA | Support for **JARs signed with ML-DSA** |
| 2027-01-19 | 21, 17 | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange for **TLS 1.3** |

The change log on the slide says the hybrid key exchange target was **narrowed from 2027-H1 to 2027-01-19** on 2026-11-05. So: JDK 25 has hybrid TLS 1.3 key exchange now, and JDK 21 and 17 are due in the January 2027 update.

One caveat, written on the roadmap itself: it is *"a summary of Oracle's current plans"* that *"can change with little or no warning,"* and it applies to the **security providers shipped in the Oracle JRE and JDK**, not to third-party providers. If you run another OpenJDK distribution, check its own release notes.

### It should be mostly a configuration change

![Slide 16: Oracle blog 'Post-Quantum Cryptography in Long-Term Support JDK Releases', August 6, 2026, with the executive summary bullets.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-pqc-lts-blog.jpg)

The next slide pointed to an Oracle blog post, *Post-Quantum Cryptography in Long-Term Support JDK Releases* (6 August 2026, by Aurelio Garcia-Ribeyro, Senior Director of Product Management): [blogs.oracle.com/java/post-quantum-cryptography-in-long-term-support-jdk-releases](https://blogs.oracle.com/java/post-quantum-cryptography-in-long-term-support-jdk-releases). The key points, as on the slide:

- Oracle is bringing PQC readiness **across supported Oracle JDK LTS releases: 25, 21, 17, 11 and 8**, not only to new JDKs.
- The goal is to **minimise application disruption**: *"Applications that simply rely on Java's TLS stack can take advantage of PQC-enabled benefits simply through updating and configuration changes."*
- Beyond PQC-enabled TLS, other cryptographic changes are part of the roadmap.
- *"Security readiness requires a continuous process; it is not a one-time goal."*

That is the message I would put on a poster for every platform team: if your services use the standard JDK TLS stack, you probably do not need a rewrite. You need to **know your JDK versions, patch regularly, and test the new settings before they become defaults**.

## What's next for you

Her closing slide has two lines, and I cannot improve on them:

1. **Audit your encrypted assets.** Take care of your application by **updating it to a recent JDK**.
2. **Don't assume encrypted = safe (forever).**

My own checklist, based on the roadmap:

- **Inventory your JDKs.** Which services run 17, 21 or 25, and on which update? The released changes need **25.0.5+9**, **21.0.13+10** or **17.0.21+9** (or later).
- **Prioritise long-lived secrets.** Data with a long confidentiality lifetime is the target of *harvest now, decrypt later*, so its TLS paths go first.
- **Test before the defaults change.** The roadmap says Oracle encourages users to test and enable changes *before* they are on by default. Put the January 2027 update for 21 and 17 in your calendar, try hybrid key exchange in staging, and watch the handshakes with JFR.
- **Keep crypto-agility as the real goal:** algorithms in configuration, not in code, so the next migration is an update and not a project.
