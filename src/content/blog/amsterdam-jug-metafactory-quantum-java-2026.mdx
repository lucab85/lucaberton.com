The closing talk at the Amsterdam JUG meetup at **Metafactory** was *"Securing Java for the Quantum Era"* by **Ana-Maria Mihalceanu**, Senior Java Developer Advocate at Oracle. It was, she said, her first security talk. It did not show: it went from number theory to a live TLS handshake to the exact JDK update you need, in 45 minutes. (The earlier talks: OpenJCockpit, an in-house AI orchestrator, and Capability Mesh.) The deck is public: [Securing Java for the Quantum Era (PDF)](https://github.com/ammbra/ammbra/blob/main/Securing%20Java%20for%20the%20Quantum%20Era.pdf).

![Ana-Maria Mihalceanu at the standing desk next to a screen showing the Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap, 2026-11-05 update.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-ana-maria-roadmap.jpg)

She opened with a question most of the room recognised: who got an email this year saying *"your personal data was accessed by a third party"*? Most of us did. We comfort ourselves that the data was **encrypted**. The talk is about why that comfort has an expiry date, and what Java gives you to do about it.

## Why today's crypto is safe

Public-key cryptography relies on maths that is **easy to compute and not feasible to reverse** on classical computers. RSA is the example: multiplying two huge primes is easy, factoring the result is not.

- An RSA-2048 number is about **617 decimal digits** long; each hidden prime has roughly **309 digits**.
- Guessing the right factor is on the order of **1 in 10<sup>300</sup>**.
- The public factoring record (RSA-896, a 270-digit number, in 2026, per her slide) is still far away from that.

One detail I did not expect: she has given this talk several times since August, and **the factoring record has moved in that time**, helped by capable AI models in the hands of capable people. Still far from today's key sizes, but moving.

## Harvest now, decrypt later

*Should we worry?* Her answer: **attackers can be patient.** If they cannot decrypt your traffic today, they can store it and decrypt it later, when more computing power is available. Data encrypted a few years ago with what was then the standard is exactly the target. If your data must stay secret for ten years, the threat is already here.

## What a quantum computer breaks, and what it only weakens

| Quantum algorithm | What it does | Effect | Affected |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Shor's** | Efficiently solves integer factorisation and discrete logarithms | **Effectively breaks** modern asymmetric cryptography | RSA, Diffie-Hellman, ECDH, DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA |
| **Grover's** | Accelerates unstructured search, including brute-force key search | **Weakens** symmetric crypto and hashing, but does not break them | AES, SHA-2, SHA-3 |

So the urgent work is on **key exchange and signatures**. Symmetric encryption and hashes need larger parameters, not a replacement.

## Governments are setting deadlines

*"Post-quantum cryptography is no longer just a research objective."*

| Location | Mandate / roadmap | Deadline (per the slide) |
|---|---|---|
| United States | Executive Order 14412, "Securing the Nation Against Advanced Cryptographic Attacks" | High-risk systems by **2030-2031**; broader federal migration by **2035** |
| European Union | EU PQC Migration Roadmap | Critical infrastructure by **2030** |
| United Kingdom | NCSC PQC Migration Timeline | Complete migration by **2035** |
| Australia | ASD Information Security Manual | Complete PQC transition by the end of **2030** |

For context: [Executive Order 14412](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/25/2026-12909/securing-the-nation-against-advanced-cryptographic-attacks) was signed on 22 June 2026. [Cloudflare's analysis](https://blog.cloudflare.com/post-quantum-eo-2026) reads it as post-quantum **key establishment** by the end of 2030 and post-quantum **authentication** (signatures and certificates) by the end of 2031. Ana-Maria also noted that these timelines have **shifted** over the years. For European companies, the line to remember is **critical infrastructure by 2030**.

## The standards: HPKE and the NIST FIPS family

**Hybrid Public Key Encryption (HPKE)**, RFC 9180, lets a sender encrypt messages of any length to a receiver's public key, and works with pre-quantum or post-quantum building blocks. Her one-line summary was **HPKE = KDF + KEM + AEAD**: a key derivation function, a key encapsulation mechanism, and authenticated encryption with additional data.

Then the three NIST standards, published in **August 2024**:

| Standard | What it is | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| **FIPS 203** (ML-KEM) | Module-lattice key encapsulation | General encryption, key exchange |
| **FIPS 204** (ML-DSA) | Module-lattice digital signatures | The primary signature standard |
| **FIPS 205** (SLH-DSA) | Stateless hash-based signatures | Signing firmware, software and code, especially for **long-lived devices** such as smart cards and embedded systems |

## How the JDK got here

| JDK | JEP | What |
|---|---|---|
| 21 | **JEP 452** | Key Encapsulation Mechanism API (security providers asked for a standard KEM API, the "KEM" in HPKE) |
| 24 | **JEP 478** | Key Derivation Function API (preview) |
| 24 | **JEP 496 / 497** | ML-KEM and ML-DSA |
| 25 | **JEP 510** | Key Derivation Function API, final |
| 27 | **JEP 527** | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange for TLS 1.3 |

[JEP 527](https://openjdk.org/jeps/527) is the step that matters for most applications. It adds three hybrid groups to the JDK's TLS 1.3 implementation, `X25519MLKEM768`, `SecP256r1MLKEM768` and `SecP384r1MLKEM1024`, and puts **`X25519MLKEM768` first in the default list**. By default, a JDK client sends two key shares, one hybrid and one classic `x25519`, so it still works with servers that do not support hybrid yet. You can change the list with `jdk.tls.namedGroups` or `SSLParameters.setNamedGroups`. Two caveats from the talk and the JEP: **both endpoints must support hybrid** for it to be used, and the IETF specifications are still drafts.

The nice part, as Ana-Maria put it: applications that use the **standard Java TLS API** get this through a **runtime upgrade**, not a code change.

## The demo: hybrid TLS, mutual TLS and JFR

*"Let's see these changes in action."* The demo app was **Spring PetClinic**, the well-known sample, with **TLS and mutual TLS** added through a Spring Boot **SSL bundle** (a keystore and a truststore).

- **Certificates.** She generated her own certificate authority and certificates, mostly with `keytool`, which ships with the JDK and creates key pairs, CSRs and the rest. One gap: `keytool` could not extract the new private key she needed for signing, so that one step used **OpenSSL**. And the usual warning: a self-made CA is for development only, never for anything that sees the internet. The keystore passwords were passed as environment variables, not shown on screen.
- **Observing the handshake.** The classic way is the `javax.net.debug` SSL logging: great for debugging, far too verbose for production. The better way is **JDK Flight Recorder**: a custom `.jfc` configuration that enables the security events (TLS handshake, X.509 certificate validation, certificates in use, security properties).
- **What the handshake showed.** The browser's *ClientHello* allowed TLS 1.2 as a minimum and listed its preferred key shares, **hybrid `X25519MLKEM768` first**, classic `X25519` right after. The server chose **TLS 1.3**, a cipher suite from the client's list, and the **hybrid group**. The hybrid key share is visibly larger.
- **Seeing it in JFR.** The TLS handshake event records the **named group**, so you can prove that a connection negotiated the hybrid group without turning on debug logging. (The Quality Outreach heads-up below documents this for JDK 28.)

She also showed a JFR feature that has nothing to do with quantum computers and everything to do with real leaks: the **initial environment variable** events would have captured her keystore passwords in the recording. Before, you had to `jfr scrub` the file afterwards. In **JDK 27**, JFR can **redact sensitive values at recording time**: keys that look like passwords and secrets are redacted by default, and you can add your own filters. (This is [JEP 536](https://openjdk.org/jeps/8372760), in-process data redaction, configured through `-XX:FlightRecorderOptions`; check the JDK 27 documentation for the exact option names.)

## The roadmap for the LTS releases you actually run

Upstream features are nice, but most enterprises run **LTS releases**. The *Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap* (2026-11-05 update) at [java.com/en/jre-jdk-cryptoroadmap.html](https://www.java.com/en/jre-jdk-cryptoroadmap.html) shows the backports.

**Already released (2026-10-20)**

| Releases affected | Algorithm | What was added |
|---|---|---|
| **25.0.5+9** | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange | Hybrid key exchange for **TLS 1.3** |
| **25.0.5+9** | PBMAC1 | PKCS12 KeyStore support for PBMAC1 |
| **21.0.13+10**, **17.0.21+9** | ML-KEM | Key Encapsulation Mechanism |
| **21.0.13+10**, **17.0.21+9** | ML-DSA | Digital Signature Algorithm |

**Planned (target 2027-01-19)**

![Slide 14: Oracle JRE and JDK Cryptographic Roadmap, planned changes table: ML-DSA signed JARs for 25, 21, 17 and post-quantum hybrid key exchange for TLS 1.3 for 21 and 17.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-crypto-roadmap-planned.jpg)

| Releases | Algorithm | Planned action |
|---|---|---|
| 25, 21, 17 | ML-DSA | Support for **JARs signed with ML-DSA** |
| 21, 17 | Post-quantum hybrid key exchange | Hybrid key exchange for **TLS 1.3** (target narrowed from 2027-H1 to 2027-01-19) |

So: JDK 25 has hybrid TLS 1.3 since the October update, and 21 and 17 are due in January 2027. Two caveats. The roadmap is *"a summary of Oracle's current plans"* that *"can change with little or no warning."* And it covers **Oracle's** JDK: other vendors usually follow, but it is up to each vendor. If your distribution differs, Ana-Maria's advice was simple: **talk to your vendor.**

The background is an Oracle blog post from August, *[Post-Quantum Cryptography in Long-Term Support JDK Releases](https://blogs.oracle.com/java/post-quantum-cryptography-in-long-term-support-jdk-releases)*: PQC readiness across Oracle JDK LTS releases **25, 21, 17, 11 and 8**, with the goal that *"applications that simply rely on Java's TLS stack can take advantage of PQC-enabled benefits simply through updating and configuration changes."*

## Bigger keys, slower handshakes?

A fair concern in the security community: hybrid key shares are **much larger** than classic elliptic-curve ones. Will they slow everything down? The JDK team has benchmarked it, and keeps making the code faster with JDK **intrinsics** (see the links below).

## From the Q&A

- **Are post-quantum keys more secure because they are bigger?** No. They are bigger because the maths is different: lattice problems instead of elliptic curves. Comparing key sizes across families is not a fair comparison.
- **What if AI breaks the hard problems?** Current algorithms resist today's attacks, AI-assisted or not, and recent AI-driven progress was guided by very knowledgeable humans. Her more worrying observation: many real breaches start with something *"childish"*, like developers keeping passwords in an internal GitHub repository, where agents then read them.
- **How do I convince my manager to make time for this?** Show them the clock: there are public countdowns to the 2030 deadlines, and governments are turning the standards into requirements. Sooner or later every company deals with a government, a regulated customer or both.

Her final advice was about attitude: **don't panic at headlines.** A reported vulnerability usually comes with a fix, so patch calmly. A **breach** is another story.

## What's next for you

![Ana-Maria Mihalceanu next to the 'What's Next for You?' slide: audit your encrypted assets, update to a recent JDK, don't assume encrypted equals safe forever.](/blog/events/amsterdam-jug-metafactory-quantum-java-2026/jug-metafactory-whats-next.jpg)

Her closing slide has three lines:

1. **Audit your encrypted assets.**
2. **Take care of your application by updating it to a recent JDK.**
3. **Don't assume encrypted = safe (forever).**

My own checklist, based on the roadmap:

- **Inventory your JDKs.** Which services run 17, 21 or 25, on which update? The released changes need **25.0.5+9**, **21.0.13+10** or **17.0.21+9** or later.
- **Prioritise long-lived secrets.** Their TLS paths go first.
- **Test before the defaults change.** Put the January 2027 update for 21 and 17 in your calendar, try hybrid key exchange in staging, and check the negotiated group with JFR.
- **Check both ends.** Hybrid only happens when client and server both support it, including load balancers, proxies and service meshes in between.
- **Keep crypto-agility as the goal:** algorithms in configuration, not in code.
