## Arriving at the Park of Sculpture

The **Parco della Scultura in Architettura** in San Donà di Piave, Italy is not your typical conference venue. Instead of hotel ballrooms and convention centers, End Summer Camp {22} transforms this architectural sculpture park into a living laboratory for hacking, free software, and DIY culture.

This year marks my first End Summer Camp, and the week (September 1–6, 2026) was a crash course in what happens when 500+ hackers, makers, developers, and tinkerers take over a space for seven days with no corporate sponsors and no keynote stages — just organic collaboration.

![Selfie with the ESC escape-key banners at the entrance](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-selfie-outdoor.jpeg)

The venue itself is a work of art — industrial yellow window frames, concrete and brick walls, and banners announcing the event in both English and Italian. The "esc" cube on the orange and white banners is everywhere, a reminder that this is a place where keyboard culture meets physical space.

## The Selfie That Captures Everything

Walking the floor on Friday afternoon, I captured a moment that says everything about the ESC spirit: hands raised in the air, the crowd in matching purple t-shirts, the mix of serious hardware on the tables and pure joy in the room.

![The crowd raises their hands during a session — purple shirts, Rubik's cubes, laptops, and good vibes](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-group-selfie-1.jpeg)

This wasn't a passive audience. The "VOID" sign on the cork board, the bulletin boards covered in flyers, the inflatable unicorn head, the wooden crocodile cutout on the shelf — everything was built by participants, for participants.

![A second angle on the crowd — purple t-shirts everywhere, the projector setup, and the collaborative energy](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-group-selfie-2.jpeg)

## My Talk: FLOSS in Nuclear Physics

I presented **["Come lavorare in fisica nucleare usando sw FLOSS"](https://pretalx.endsummer.camp/2k26/talk/KCCW3Q/)** (How to work in nuclear physics using FLOSS) on Friday, September 4 at 4:15 PM in the Main Hall (CrunchLab).

![On stage at the ESC podium, microphone in hand — the wooden podium with the red ESC cube logo, Disaronno bottle, and power strips tell their own story](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-talk-1.jpeg)

My talk traced the software tools I've used throughout my career in nuclear physics (since ~2009), organized around two physical systems I've worked on recently:

1. **Atomic Beam Source** — from chopper speed controllers programmed with PIC microcontrollers (fully open chain since ~2008) to FreeCAD/CAM simulations, RRDTool live graphs, and QElectrotech for vacuum system schematics
2. **RICH prototype for the Electron Ion Collider (BNL)** — using FreeCAD as a GEANT replacement, FPGA-based remote triggers, revitalizing old Motif software with qcad/kvm, LabPlot for data analysis, Raspberry Pi pairs for data acquisition at Jefferson Lab

![Speaking at the ESC podium — gesturing with the mic in hand, the workshop space behind me](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-talk-2.jpeg)

The recurring theme: every piece of software I touched was free/open source. No proprietary licenses, no vendor lock-in, no budget approvals — just the freedom to understand, modify, and extend every tool. When you're working with million-dollar particle detectors, that freedom isn't just ideological — it's practical engineering.

![Mid-presentation gesture — the grey shelving, the "CHIODI T.P." box, and the industrial workshop setting](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-talk-3.jpeg)

## The Audio Setup: Making Sound

One of the most striking things about ESC is how it becomes a city — with its own sound system, lighting, and infrastructure, all built by participants.

![The Behringer Xenyx X1222USB mixer at ESC — 12 channels of audio chaos, taped faders and all](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-audio-setup.jpeg)

The audio setup — a Behringer Xenyx X1222USB in a road case with an HP monitor perched on top — is held together with colored tape on the faders, a USB Wi-Fi adapter, and the kind of cable management that only works because the person who wired it knows every path by heart. This is the anti-corporate-AV aesthetic: functional, chaotic, and deeply personal.

![The modular synthesizer corner — LYRA-8 organic synthesizer, Korg Volca Beats, and a tangle of cables](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-audio-equipment.jpeg)

The music corner was a hacker's dream: a LYRA-8 organic synthesizer alongside Korg Volcas, tangled cables, and a pink balloon that had seen better days. Someone had set up a full DIY music production station, and it was humming through the afternoon.

## The Infrastructure Room: Networks and Servers

![The red equipment cabinet — MCBs, terminal blocks, network switches, and patch panels inside](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-cabinet.jpeg)

Behind the creative chaos is the infrastructure that keeps it all running: a red 19-inch cabinet with circuit breakers, terminal blocks, network switches, and patch panels. This is the nervous system of ESC — the network that connects workshop tables to the projector, the PA system, and the shared filesystem where all the session materials live.

## The Crowd and the Culture

What struck me most about ESC — compared to corporate conferences I've attended — is the absence of hierarchy. No VIP stages. No sponsored tracks. No "industry experts" on pedestals. Instead, there's a room full of people in purple t-shirts raising their hands in cheer, a cork board full of flyers, a wooden crocodile cutout on a shelf, and an inflatable unicorn head watching over it all.

![The crowd at the ESC venue — the workshop space with patio chairs, projection screen, and the "METRO" shirts on the wall](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-crowd-workshop.jpeg)

![The dining area at ESC — a long checkered table loaded with food, people sharing meals, and the "TRUST" purple t-shirts visible in the background](/blog/events/end-summer-camp-2026/esc-2026-group-selfie-5.jpeg)

The "VOID" sign and the bulletin boards tell you everything: this is a space that participants fill, not one that's pre-decorated. The banners, the flyers, the clothes hanging on the wall — everything is added by whoever shows up and wants to contribute.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Participant-driven content works.** The schedule is created by proposals that emerge from the community — and the quality of talks, labs, and workshops is consistently high because people are presenting on topics they genuinely care about.

2. **The DIY infrastructure is impressive.** From the audio system wired with colored tape to the red network cabinet with hand-labeled patch panels, ESC proves you don't need enterprise AV vendors to create a functional event space.

3. **FLOSS isn't just software — it's a mindset.** The emphasis on free/open-source tools extends beyond code: the event itself runs on open principles, from the transparent budget to the volunteer-run organization.

4. **Physical space matters.** The Parco della Scultura provides a unique backdrop — industrial concrete, exposed brick, yellow window frames — that feels like a factory converted into a hackerspace, which is exactly what it becomes for a week.

## My Take

After a week at ESC, I understand why this event has run for 22 editions. It's not about the talks, the workshops, or even the projects. It's about creating a space where curiosity is the only credential, where the person in the purple t-shirt raising their hand could be the one who just reverse-engineered a MIFARE card or built a LoRa mesh from scratch.

The escape key on the banners isn't just a logo — it's an invitation to escape from the constraints of professional software, corporate conference formats, and the assumption that innovation requires funding. At ESC, you need curiosity, a willingness to learn, and maybe some colored tape for the audio mixer.
