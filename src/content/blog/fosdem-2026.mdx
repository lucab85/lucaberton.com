## FOSDEM 2026 — Still Buzzing!

Just back from **FOSDEM 2026** in Brussels — and the energy was incredible. **8,000+ open source minds**, **65 beers**, **1,013 talks**… and enough hallway track conversations to fuel a year's worth of ideas.

## RISC-V & Open Silicon

This year, I had the chance to connect deeply with the teams from **DeepComputing** and the **RISC-V International Foundation**, and I must say: the momentum around **open silicon** and **custom compute** is real.

Seeing the energy in the **RISC-V devroom** and chatting about **hardware/software co-design** reminded me how far open innovation has come — and where it's going.

### Key Takeaways

- **RISC-V is getting real traction** for AI at the edge
- **Hardware is getting more open** — and that unlocks a new class of innovation
- Still amazed by how **strong and welcoming** the open source community is

## Saturday Evening: Robots on the Dinner Table

The best demo of Saturday didn't happen in a devroom. Over dinner that evening, fellow attendees at our table unpacked small cube robots. The box read **[CoCube](https://www.cocubefun.com/)**, "Tabletop AI Robot for Education", and the robots were programmed in **[MicroBlocks](https://microblocks.fun/)**, a Scratch-inspired blocks language for physical computing.

The first demo, explained in Italian, was football. Each robot always knows its exact X and Y coordinates on the printed pitch, and its direction is a vector. So the program just tells it where the goal is, points it at those coordinates and shoots. "I'll score every time", as the presenter put it. Next step: two robots playing each other, each programmed from its own laptop.

The second demo turned a printed piano mat into an instrument. In the presenter's explanation, the robot has a small infrared camera that reads a pattern of tiny dots printed across the mat, so every key works like a code. The camera ignores colour, so you can print your own football pitch, maze or race track and colour it however you like, as long as the dots are there. They printed theirs on an ordinary printer at 1200 DPI. In MicroBlocks, tapping an instrument toggles it in or out of the tune: snare drum, hi-hat, bass, trumpet. Add-on modules click onto the cube, such as a distance sensor and a MIDI module, plus one that simply exposes the wires so you can attach your own sensors or actuators over I²C. According to the presenter, the robot was developed by a PhD student in China, and more modules are on the way.

The presenter, an electrical engineer by training, compared it with programming seven-segment displays in assembly back in the day: here you just give the block the number and it shows up. FOSDEM Junior even had a hands-on workshop on programming these [tabletop football robots with MicroBlocks in 2025](https://archive.fosdem.org/2025/schedule/event/fosdem-2025-4696-learn-to-program-tabletop-football-playing-robots/).

My take: this is what good teaching hardware looks like. Positioning, sensors and buses are abstracted just enough that a beginner gets a goal or a drum beat in minutes, while the I²C escape hatch keeps the real electronics within reach.
