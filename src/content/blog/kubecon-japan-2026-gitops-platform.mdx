While the headline at KubeCon + CloudNativeCon Japan 2026 was AI, the **platform engineering** track was where the day-to-day Kubernetes work actually gets done. Two talks I caught sum up where the discipline is heading: the move from imperative Helm to GitOps, and operating Argo CD at serious scale.

## From Helm shell scripts to GitOps

One session laid out the before/after cleanly. The legacy approach was an imperative command:

> `helm upgrade --install sample-app ./charts/sample-app --namespace sample --create-namespace`

The slide called out the real costs of that pattern: **no declarative management, limited reproducibility, no standardized operations, and environment drift.**

The fix was GitOps: define a declarative release (a Helmfile snippet), open it as a pull request, and let **Argo CD** watch the Git repository, detect the change, and reconcile the cluster. Git becomes the source of truth, every change is reviewable, and "drift" stops being something you discover at 3 a.m.

## Argo CD at scale: 5,000+ applications, 300+ clusters

A separate talk, **"One Argo CD,"** described running a single Argo CD instance across **5,000+ applications** and **300+ clusters**. At that size, the interesting problems are operational, not conceptual:

- **Sharded controllers** to spread reconciliation load.
- **Jitter tuning** so thousands of applications don't all sync at once.
- **Processor tuning** to keep the controller responsive.

It is a useful counterpoint to the "just install Argo CD" advice. GitOps scales beautifully — until your controller is trying to reconcile five thousand apps, at which point the tuning *is* the job.

## Platform engineering in practice: LY Corporation

The platform-engineering thread also showed up in a deep dive from **Shota Yoshimura, Senior Platform Engineer at LY Corporation**, titled *"Scaling Kubernetes Safely on On-Prem KaaS Across 1,300+ Clusters and 40,000+ Nodes."*

The core challenge: on-prem capacity is not elastic. Hardware procurement and rack capacity are real constraints, unlike the cloud. His team built a custom controller to consolidate underutilized worker nodes safely — using seven-day peak metrics and careful DaemonSet termination ordering — to reclaim capacity without creating long-term operational debt.

That is platform engineering in a sentence: treating your cluster capacity as a product your internal customers can rely on, even when the underlying hardware is fixed.

## Takeaway

The AI talks get the attention, but the GitOps and platform-engineering sessions are where teams learn to *operate* what the AI talks assume. Declarative everything, Git as the source of truth, and controllers tuned for your actual scale — that is the unglamorous foundation KubeCon Japan 2026 kept pointing back to.
