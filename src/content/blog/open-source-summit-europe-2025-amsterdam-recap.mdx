The Linux Foundation's **Open Source Summit Europe 2025** ran at RAI Amsterdam from Monday 25 to Wednesday 27 August, co-located with the **Embedded Linux Conference Europe** (ELC). I was there all three days. I sat in the keynote hall in the mornings, recorded short interviews in the Solutions Showcase, and spent the afternoons in ELC rooms. I was also there supporting [DeepComputing](/blog/deepcomputing-dc-roma-riscv-deepseek-local-ai-2026/) at the RISC-V booth, which is why I'm wearing their T-shirt in several of these photos.

I'm writing this in October 2026, a few days before the 2026 edition opens in Prague, so it's a look back. Everything below comes from the slides I photographed, the talks and interviews I recorded, and the official announcements I checked afterwards. All times are local (CEST).

## Day 1 keynotes: agents, databases and money

The opening keynote set the theme with a slide that read **"2025 is the Era of AI Agents (Agentic AI)"** and, underneath, "Frontier Models are so 2024". The next slide covered DeepSeek's V3.1 release as a pivot towards agentic, tool-using tasks.

The most useful slide for me was **"The Agent 'stack'"**. From top to bottom:

- **Agent Experience/UX**: conversational and action interfaces
- **Agent Orchestrators**: Azure AI Foundry, Google Vertex, Amazon Bedrock Agents, LangChain, CrewAI
- **Protocols & Standards** (MCP, A2A) next to **Registries** (secure discovery and orchestration)
- **Skills/Tools Plugins**: from API management to enterprise resources

On the left an arrow pointed down: "OSS Quickly Pushes Cost Down". On the right an arrow pointed up: "Value and Innovation Go Up".

![The Agent stack keynote slide at Open Source Summit Europe 2025, layering agent UX, orchestrators, protocols and registries, and tools, with OSS pushing cost down and value going up](/blog/events/open-source-summit-europe-2025-amsterdam-recap/agent-stack-keynote.jpg)

*"The Agent 'stack'": open source at the bottom pushing cost down, value moving up the layers.*

The keynote also brought a reality check. A slide citing a University of Texas at Austin study said early studies show "higher personal productivity but no major project-level breakthroughs yet". According to the slide, Copilot raised OSS project-level contributions by **5.9%** (+2.1% individual contributions, +3.4% developer participation), while coordination and integration time went up by **8%**. In other words, more and broader contributions, but slower coordination.

My take: that tradeoff matches what I see with platform teams. AI assistants make it cheap to open a pull request. Reviewing, integrating and owning the change still costs the same human time, and that's where the backlog moves.

### agentgateway: why not just Envoy?

agentgateway got its own short keynote from the team at Solo.io, which [contributed the project](https://agentgateway.dev/blog/2025-08-25-solo-contributes-agentgateway-to-lf). I recorded it. The argument went like this:

- Agents work best when they're small and focused, with a small set of tools. Bigger workflows come from agents passing state and tasks to each other.
- MCP (for tools) and A2A (between agents) are both JSON-RPC, but with long-lived, stateful, multi-turn sessions. They help with interoperability, but they don't solve governance, security, policy, observability, rate limiting or cost control.
- Envoy was built for classic HTTP: read the headers, stream the body, close the connection. AI traffic needs to look deep inside the body. So they built a new proxy from scratch, **in Rust**.

The speaker described three use cases:

- **Consuming LLMs** as an egress gateway, with model failover, guardrails injected into prompts, and credential management.
- **Self-hosted inference routing**, using signals like GPU and cache utilisation, LoRA adapters and queue depth.
- **An MCP gateway**, with tool-server aggregation, authorisation, protocol-aware routing and deep tracing, so that not every agent can reach every tool.

It runs standalone, with a UI, declarative config and a playground. On Kubernetes, a control plane such as kgateway or Istio can program it live over xDS.

![Welcome to the Linux Foundation: agentgateway keynote slide at Open Source Summit Europe 2025, describing the first data plane purpose-built for AI agents](/blog/events/open-source-summit-europe-2025-amsterdam-recap/agentgateway-joins-linux-foundation.jpg)

*agentgateway joining the Linux Foundation, Monday morning.*

My take: the "support agent accidentally calls the payroll system" example from this talk is the real reason to put a gateway in front of MCP servers. Scoping which agent sees which tools is an authorisation problem, and it belongs in the platform, not in each agent's prompt.

The morning closed with a **DevRel Foundation** spotlight from **Stacey Kruczek** (introduced on the slide as Community Growth Strategist and Developer Relations Leader). Under "Scaling the Challenges of DevRel: is the profession at risk?", the slide quoted the 11th annual State of DevRel survey (2024): 50% find awareness of their impact challenging, and 61% find it hard to meet business impact.

### Helen, openEuler community

On Wednesday I came back to openEuler and recorded a short interview with **Helen** from the openEuler community. She described openEuler as a fully open source, Linux-based operating system run by the OpenAtom Foundation, with a growing set of AI features for developers. The one she highlighted was **DevStation**, a desktop edition you can download from the official site. It comes with an AI agent out of the box, so you can ask the OS in natural language to handle development tasks. The [openEuler DevStation docs](https://docs.openeuler.org/en/docs/24.03_LTS_SP2/devstation/) describe it as the project's first workstation built for developers.

### James, RISC-V International

At the RISC-V booth on Tuesday I interviewed **James**, who said his job at RISC-V International is to develop content: telling the stories of what people build with RISC-V. He compared the development boards on the table to an Arduino or Raspberry Pi kit. They ship with documentation, SDKs, compilers and pre-built binaries, because the goal is to "reduce the time to value" between a board arriving and someone building something with it.

His main point was **RVA23**. In the past, he said, many RISC-V boards targeted different versions of the ISA, so you never knew whether a Linux distribution would boot out of the box. RVA23 gives everyone one stable hardware target. He said Canonical and Red Hat were already building RVA23-compatible Ubuntu and RHEL, so that when RVA23 hardware rolled off production lines (he expected the end of 2025 or early 2026), it would just work. Individuals can join the community for free through riscv.org, and companies join as paid members. I wrote a separate explainer on [what RVA23 means](/blog/risc-v-profiles-rva23-explained/).

## Day 2 keynotes: Microsoft, OpenSearch and drones

Tuesday opened with an **openEuler** keynote from the chair of the project, about its AI stack. I caught the end. He described two newer acceleration projects. One splits prefill and decode across different nodes, including CPUs, and he quoted 10–30% higher throughput depending on the model. The other sorts MoE experts into hot, warm and cold, keeps the cold ones on CPU and the hot ones on GPU, and he quoted a 10–15% throughput gain. He was candid that nobody yet knew what happens when you combine the two. He also shared a naming tradition: openEuler releases are named after a food. He picked the first one, *hui mian*, a noodle dish from his home province of Henan, and invited the audience to suggest the next.

Next was **Ryan Waite** of Microsoft (Open-Source Ecosystems and Open-Source Incubations, according to his title slide) with **"Open Source & AI at the Core: How Microsoft Builds and Runs on OSS"**. He opened by admitting that 15 to 20 years ago Microsoft was, "if you were polite", a sceptic. He traced the change from a contribution of about 20,000 lines of code in 2009, through VS Code, to the GitHub acquisition in 2018. Some claims from his talk:

- Microsoft is the leading cloud contributor to the CNCF and the number two contributor overall for the last three years.
- More than two thirds of the virtual machines in Azure run Linux.
- The company works "upstream first", then brings those changes downstream into its products.
- Security comes first, with new open source projects such as Copacetic and Dalec, and partnerships with the Rust Foundation, Alpha-Omega and the OpenSSF.

Then **Pallavi Priyadarshini** gave **"OpenSearch at the Linux Foundation: one year of innovation and community growth"**. According to her talk:

- OpenSearch 3.0 shipped in May 2025, and 3.2 came out the week before the summit, on an eight-week release cadence.
- The project has passed 3,000 contributors.
- 3.2 is "11x more performant" than OpenSearch 1.0.
- Contributors include Uber, ByteDance (conversational search), SAP (security features), NVIDIA (GPU acceleration for vector search) and engineers from Apple.
- New in the 3.x line: agentic search, agentic memory and native MCP support.
- The 3.2 release was orchestrated end to end by an AI release bot.

## Embedded Linux Conference: boot time, Bluetooth at scale and cameras

On Tuesday afternoon at 15:05 I looked into **[The Cost of Security: Measuring and Reducing Boot-Time Impact](https://osseu2025.sched.com/event/25Vso/the-cost-of-security-measuring-and-reducing-boot-time-impact-michael-olbrich-pengutronix)**. The room was packed. The slide on screen explained how to measure boot time:

- timestamps from the serial console output
- kernel timestamps (clock monotonic)
- the offset between the bootloader starting the kernel and timestamp zero of clock monotonic, which was 220 ms in that example

I spent the rest of the slot next door at **[Testing Zephyr Bluetooth Devices at Scale](https://osseu2025.sched.com/event/25Vsu/testing-zephyr-bluetooth-devices-at-scale-donatien-garnier-blecon-ltd)** by **Donatien Garnier** of Blecon. From the slides:

- **Early insights**: timing benchmarks (medians) comparing Android GATT and iOS L2CAP, and charts of advertising packets lost between test devices and gateways.
- **Early hardware-in-the-loop testing** with balena managing nRF development kits.
- **A testbed** of 7 × 16 nRF52840 dongles running dedicated firmware, updated nightly from main. One callout warned about USB resource exhaustion.
- **Metrics and telemetry**: connection and receive times per device, exported through OpenTelemetry.
- **Zephyr's `native_sim`**: `west build -b native_sim` uses the host's Bluetooth adapter as the controller.
- **Fast reference scenario testing** with RootCanal, a virtual controller connected over HCI + TCP to native_sim, Cuttlefish and a hotspot.
- **Conclusion**: "Divide and conquer", "First things first", "Focus on what's important".

![Blecon talk at Embedded Linux Conference Europe 2025: Early Hardware-In-the-Loop testing slide with balena and nRF development kits, speaker at the podium](/blog/events/open-source-summit-europe-2025-amsterdam-recap/blecon-hardware-in-the-loop.jpg)

*Blecon's hardware-in-the-loop setup: balena-managed nRF boards in the test loop.*

My take: this is the same layered approach good platform teams use for Kubernetes. Simulate first (native_sim and RootCanal, like kind clusters), run a cheap hardware farm nightly (like ephemeral test clusters), and keep real-world characterisation for the few questions only real hardware can answer. Exporting device metrics through OpenTelemetry means embedded test results end up in the same observability stack as everything else.

Later on Tuesday I caught part of the **libcamera** update. One slide introduced **Camshark**, "a GUI tool for libcamera" with full access to all camera features, working remotely over SSH and with heterogeneous cameras. The [libcamera ELCE 2025 post](https://libcamera.org/entries/2025-08-25.html) has the full list of demos.

## Day 3 keynotes: vertical foundations and uncertainty

Wednesday morning was about industries. A FINOS (Fintech Open Source Foundation) keynote by its chair emeritus described the **AI governance framework**, a crowdsourced framework he said banks would traditionally have paid consultants to build. He also covered **Common Cloud Controls**, a Citi-led project on oversight of multiple cloud providers, which he tied to Europe's digital sovereignty debate. FINOS announced eight new members and a new chair from RBC. The call-to-action slide said "remember, contribution is not just code!"

A Linux Foundation slide then mapped **horizontal and vertical foundations**: CNCF and the Open Infra Foundation as horizontal layers, with FINOS, LF Energy and AgStack as verticals. That led into a panel with **Arpit Joshipura** (Linux Foundation), **Lingli Deng** (China Mobile) and **Madeline Dassule** (FINOS). The telecom side of the discussion described a real tension: telecom infrastructure is specified on five- to ten-year cycles, while open source iterates every six months. Because of that complexity, the expectation that open source would change the supply chain hadn't materialised: operators still depended on their main suppliers.

## Yuning Liang: RISC-V AI PCs, from the hardware side

At 14:07 on Wednesday, in an ELC room, DeepComputing founder **Yuning Liang** gave **"Hardware Innovation in the World's First RISC-V 50 TOPS AI Compute for Mass Production Development"**. Most of his deck was marked company-confidential, so I'm not publishing those slides. I'm only summarising the public points he made out loud:

- He introduced himself as "a core software guy" who used to port Java virtual machines to PowerPC and MIPS, and joked that he was "stupid enough" to spend his money making hardware.
- DeepComputing makes consumer electronics only on RISC-V: tablets, smart speakers, routers and laptops.
- He partnered with Framework so that a RISC-V mainboard can go into an upgradeable, repairable laptop. One board design can then serve several products, which helps a small company reach volume.
- For AI on RISC-V, his message was that the hard part is software: porting and optimising frameworks, models and runtimes. The day before, in our interview, he had made the same ask: more open source software developers contributing to the RISC-V ecosystem.

## Emiel Brok (SUSE): the perfect storm of NIS2, CRA and sovereignty

At 16:06, **Emiel Brok** gave **"Perfect Storm: What Digital Sovereignty, NIS2 and CRA mean for your IT Strategy"**. His slide introduced him as Open Source Ambassador at SUSE, co-founder and board member of DOSBA, and board member of APELL. On stage he explained that DOSBA is the Dutch Open Source Business Alliance, and that APELL is the European umbrella for about nine national business alliances. The talk covered:

- **The most pressing regulations**: NIS-2, the CRA (Cyber Resilience Act), DORA and PCI-DSS.
- **Who is in scope for NIS2**: lists of critical and highly critical sectors, from manufacturing and electricity to banking and digital infrastructure, with a size threshold of at least 50 employees and €10 million annual turnover.
- **Common Criteria EAL 4+ vs ISO 27001**: EAL stands for Evaluation Assurance Level, which he summed up as how deep the evaluator looks into your supply chain. He named the BSI as the independent body that checks compliance with these criteria, and the slides showed mutual recognition through SOGIS-MRA and CCRA.
- **"Unpacking Digital Sovereignty"** into seven parts: secure and independent supply chains, data governance and access control, technological sovereignty, resilience and business continuity, software autonomy and open source, regulatory compliance and privacy, and infrastructure and hardware control.
- **"Why SUSE?"**: SUSE's position on open sovereign IT, with local delivery backed by global capability.

## What I took away

- **Agents arrived at the Linux Foundation as infrastructure, not demos.** The day-1 stack slide and agentgateway were both about the boring layers: protocols, registries, gateways and policy. That's where platform teams will spend their time.
- **RISC-V's bottleneck is software, and RVA23 is the fix everyone pointed to.** James at RISC-V International and Yuning Liang said the same thing from two sides: a stable hardware target plus distributions that work out of the box.
- **Embedded testing looks more and more like cloud testing.** Simulation, nightly hardware farms and OpenTelemetry all showed up in one Zephyr talk.
- **Sovereignty is now a compliance topic.** NIS2, the CRA and DORA turned "digital sovereignty" from a keynote word into an audit checklist.
