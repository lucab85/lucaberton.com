At **Open Source Summit Europe 2026** in Prague I went in as a media partner with one goal: record as many short, focused conversations for [Udienza](https://www.udienza.com/) as three days allow. By the time I left for the airport on Friday, the calendar had held about twenty interview slots: kernel maintainers, foundation leaders, OS teams, security projects and embedded engineers.

People asked me how that works, so here is the playbook. None of it needs a big media budget. It needs preparation and a calendar you trust.

![Luca Berton and Marcin Czupryniak, the Udienza crew, on the first morning in Prague](/blog/events/open-source-summit-2026/oss-eu-2026-udienza-crew-morning.jpg)

## 1. Define the format before you ask anyone

Udienza has a fixed format, and every pitch said it in one sentence:

> "Udienza is a ten-minute video show with the people who build what the rest of us run on. No panel and no product pitch — just one focused engineering/maintainer idea on camera."

Ten minutes is the key. It is short enough that a maintainer between two sessions can say yes without thinking about it, and long enough for one real idea. When I asked for the CNCF slot, I described it as "ten minutes, one technical idea, on camera, focused on engineering decisions, trade-offs, and lessons maintainers can share with other builders."

The internal structure is just as fixed:

1. **0:00** Cold open: the guest states the one thing they built or changed.
2. **0:30** Who they are, in one line. No bio reading.
3. **1:00** The idea: what did you build, and why does it matter to someone running systems today?
4. **5:00** The hard part: what broke, what surprised you, what you would do differently.
5. **8:00** For the people who could not be here: one thing to read, try or watch.
6. **9:30** Where to find them.

## 2. Pitch one angle per person, tied to their own talk

Generic requests ("would you like to be on my podcast?") get ignored. Every pitch I sent referenced something specific the person was doing that week:

- For **Arnaldo Carvalho de Melo**, his "20 years of pahole" session at Linux Plumbers and how a developer tool became BPF infrastructure.
- For **Konstantin Ryabitsev**, his Kernel Summit session on b4's maintainer features.
- For **Franklin Cooper** at TI, his talk on FPGA-based test fabrics, which I described as "a real constraint, a design decision, and something other builders can reproduce".
- For **Mario Sánchez Prada** at Igalia, his session on the new WPEPlatform API.
- For **Erin McKean**, her keynote on documentation for humans and AI agents.

Then I added one line that matters more than it looks: *"The conversation is technical and editorial, not a product pitch."* Companies say yes faster when they know you will not ask about pricing. Engineers say yes faster when they know you will not ask about marketing.

## 3. Work with the organiser's PR team

As a media partner, I asked the Linux Foundation for four things a month before the event: 1:1 interviews, access to an interview room, a media badge for my camera operator, and ten minutes with Linus Torvalds.

What I got:

- **Introductions and booked sessions.** The LF PR team offered and booked sessions with Bob Killen and Daniel Krook of CNCF and with Bianca Lewis of OpenSearch.
- **A media registration** for Marcin Czupryniak, who filmed everything.
- **The Udienza logo on the event's sponsor page**, which made every cold email more credible.
- **An honest answer on Linus**: he chooses his own interviews and cannot reply to most requests. Fair enough.

The PR team also sent embargoed announcements ahead of the CNCF interview, so I could prepare questions on news that went public the morning of the summit.

## 4. One calendar event per guest, with everything inside

This is the part that made three days possible. Every interview became a calendar event with the same structure:

- **Status**: CONFIRMED, HOLD or UNCONFIRMED, in the title.
- **Guest**: name, role, organisation, and how they prefer to be contacted. Some people only use Signal and not WhatsApp; respect that.
- **When and where**: a concrete meeting point. "Booth 20", "Registration desk", "the TI booth".
- **Background**: three or four lines on who they are and what they built.
- **Angle**: one sentence.
- **Questions**: five to ten, numbered.
- **Ten-minute flow**: which questions to prioritise, for example "1 → 3 → 4 → 5 → 9, close with 10".

On the show floor there is no time to open a notes app. I opened the calendar event, read the angle and the first three questions, and walked to the meeting point.

## 5. Be honest about what does not work

Not everything worked, and that is normal:

- **Some people never replied.** Senior executives at large vendors have full schedules. One PR team told me exactly that, kindly.
- **Some replied with an out-of-office.** People travel to conferences and stop reading email.
- **Some guests change.** When a founder could not attend, their company proposed their VP of Engineering instead, who turned out to be a great guest.
- **Some slots overlapped.** On Friday morning I was still wrapping up one interview when the next guest was already looking for me at reception.

The fix for most of this is the same: **move the coordination to the phone on the day.** Email is for booking. On site, a quick message ("I'm at the registration desk now") saves the slot.

## 6. Leave room for the hallway

Some of the best conversations of the week were not on the calendar: people I met at booths, at the reception, or in the queue for coffee. I wrote them up in [People I Met at Open Source Summit Europe 2026](/blog/oss-europe-2026-people-i-met-prague/). A schedule with no gaps has no room for luck.
