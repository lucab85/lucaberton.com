On Wednesday 18 March 2026 I attended the **Platform Engineering Amsterdam** meetup at Tolhuistuin, one of my favorite venues in Amsterdam Noord. The theme was "Human Intelligence" — a refreshing counterpoint in a world where every event is about AI. The idea: real experiences, lessons learned, and hard-earned knowledge shared human-to-human.

The event was hosted by Darko K. and Michaela at the beautiful Zonzij room inside Tolhuistuin, sponsored by **Coder** and **Tarmac**. Around 128 attendees showed up for three talks, food, drinks, and a gorgeous sunset over the IJ.

## Talk 1: Efficiency Patterns in Agentic Software Development

**Michael Suchacz** of Coder tackled the question every platform team is asking: *How do we make AI work efficiently at scale?*

His talk covered practical approaches to optimizing agentic workflows:

- **Context management strategies** that keep models focused and reduce token waste
- **Architectural patterns** like sub-agents and swarms that distribute cognitive load across multiple specialized agents
- **Efficiency tradeoffs** — where gains are real versus illusory
- **Security considerations** when giving agents more autonomy in development environments

![Wide view of the Tolhuistuin room during the Coder talk, with the Thinking Fast and Slow slide comparing System 1 and System 2 on screen](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/coder-thinking-fast-and-slow.jpg)

*Left: the "Subagent Tradeoffs" slide, ending with "If reasoning is tightly coupled, keep it together. Decompose only when subtasks are genuinely independent." Right: the "Thinking Fast and Slow" slide on System 1 and System 2, which led into applying the idea to agents.*

This resonated strongly with my own experience building [AI-augmented infrastructure workflows](/blog/agentic-ai-infrastructure-automation/). The key takeaway: throwing more compute at agents is not the answer. Smart context windowing and task decomposition matter more than model size.

For teams exploring [GitHub Copilot Agent Mode](/blog/github-copilot-agent-mode-vscode-agentic-development/) or similar agentic tools, Michael's patterns around sub-agent orchestration are directly applicable.

### What Michael actually said: harness, loop and benchmarks

Going back through the clips I recorded, there was more substance than the summary above. Michael introduced himself as, until recently, head of engineering for **Mux**, Coder's tool for agentic software development. Coder itself builds cloud development environments and is now adding an AI layer on top. He first listed what companies already worry about when they roll out agents: compute for the agent that can't touch a developer's machine, a standard roll-out, fallbacks, automatic code review, and visibility into what people do with agents and which models they may use. Then he questioned the word "efficiency". If leadership says it will pay for whatever tokens you need, efficiency stops meaning "cheapest" and starts meaning "best possible result".

![The audience at Tolhuistuin facing the What Is an Agent Harness? slide, with the speaker at the lectern and a tarmac roll-up banner on the left](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/coder-agent-harness.jpg)

*"What Is an Agent Harness?": the loop, tool definitions and execution, context assembly and management, guardrails and validation. "The model is the engine. The harness is the car."*

**The loop.** An agent gets a task, usually a prompt, and assembles context: the AGENTS.md or CLAUDE.md files and whatever else matters. That context is all the model knows about your intent, because it has no long-term memory. Every call to the LLM API sends the entire conversation again as JSON. Caching helps, but only when you hit the cache.

**Programmatic tool calling.** Models tend to make many small tool calls: read this file, read that file, query the database once per region. Since the model can write code, the alternative is to let it write the chain of calls itself and return only the final result. According to Michael, Anthropic offers this but runs the code in its own sandbox and bills you for the minutes it runs. Mux ran it in a local sandbox instead, to cut latency and avoid round trips to the model.

**Sub-agents and System 1.** For isolated pieces of work, an orchestrator can spin up sub-agents on a faster model. In Mux, the results come back as a Git patch in email format, an old Git feature that fits the job well. The "Thinking Fast and Slow" part applied the same idea to routing: a cheap, fast model (Haiku, in the benchmarks) as System 1, and a stronger model as System 2.

**What the benchmarks showed.** Beyond the averages in the table further down, he shared some anecdotes. On one task, the only agent that passed was Claude Code on Opus, for about 57 cents. Codex spent about 8 dollars with no result, and Mux failed too. The models all made the obvious edits but missed a value buried deep in a nested JSON file. GPT-based Codex did everything it was asked "and then some more", editing and deleting things in other files. On another task it was the other way round: Claude timed out and got stuck. Cost accounting was hard in itself, because the providers report cached and uncached input tokens differently.

![Speaker at the lectern next to the What the Data Actually Shows slide, with the audience in the foreground](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/coder-what-the-data-shows.jpg)

*"What the Data Actually Shows": System 1 routing is high-variance (the Haiku variant passed 33% in run 1 and 83% in run 2), cheapest is not most efficient, and brute-force token spend can work.*

**What lasts.** His conclusion was that clever harness tricks probably won't matter much in the long term. Every time the providers add a capability, they also pull you further into their ecosystem. What stays useful is the work around the model: benchmarks that tell you whether something actually works, security boundaries and sandboxing, audit trails, a human in the loop who can see the results, and the tools and APIs your agents use.

My take: that last list is a platform team's job description. Sandboxes, audit trails, model allow-lists and an internal benchmark suite are the parts of an agent platform that survive the next model release. Prompt tricks don't.

## Talk 2: A Practical Guide to Inner Sourcing Your IDP

**Lian Li**, Cloud Native Human and experienced Developer Advocate, presented a compelling framework for treating your Internal Developer Platform as an open source project rather than just a product.

The core insight: product thinking gets you started, but **inner source thinking** gets you adoption. When developers can contribute to and maintain platform features, the gap between "what developers need" and "what the platform provides" shrinks dramatically.

Her concrete steps included:

- **Identifying contribution opportunities** — which platform components benefit most from developer input
- **Setting up maintainable processes** — contribution guidelines, review workflows, and ownership models
- **Measuring engagement** — tracking adoption, contributions, and developer satisfaction

![Lian on stage in front of the Developer Journey Stages slide showing Discover, Evaluate, Learn, Build and Scale, with the audience in the foreground](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/lian-developer-journey-stages.jpg)
*A slide from "A practical guide to inner sourcing your IDP": the Developer Journey Stages, Discover, Evaluate, Learn, Build and Scale.*

This aligns perfectly with what I see in [Backstage-based developer portals](/blog/backstage-plugins-internal-developer-portal/). The most successful IDPs are the ones where application teams feel ownership, not just consumption. Lian's framework gives platform teams a roadmap to get there.

## Talk 3: Escaping the Data Center — Migration Wins and Woes

**Dejan Mladenovski**, Principal Software Engineer at G+D Netcetera, gave the most honest talk of the evening. Three questions framed the whole presentation:

1. Why did we migrate to cloud?
2. Was it a good idea to migrate?
3. Did I at some point want to quit and move to a farm?

![Dejan Mladenovski at the lectern beside his title slide, Escaping the Data Center: Migration Wins and Woes, Principal Software Engineer, Netcetera](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/netcetera-escaping-the-data-center.jpg)
*The title slide of "Escaping the Data Center: Migration Wins and Woes" at Platform Engineering Amsterdam.*

The answer to the third question got the biggest laugh of the night — and anyone who has led a major cloud migration knows exactly why. The reality of moving enterprise workloads to the cloud is messier than any vendor presentation suggests.

For teams considering similar moves, the lessons around [cloud infrastructure strategy](/services/cloud-infrastructure/) and understanding the true cost of migration (not just the compute bill, but the organizational change) were invaluable.

### Netcetera: why they left the data center

Dejan Mladenovski's title slide read "Escaping the Data Center: Migration Wins and Woes", with the Netcetera logo. His "Why did we migrate?" slide gave the reasons in three columns:

- **Operating cost**: 400k EUR/year, plus 50k EUR/year in licences
- **Maintenance**: monthly patching, certificate renewals, hardware, software updates
- **Scalability**: scaling took day(s), and global expansion was non-existent

![Dejan Mladenovski at the lectern beside the Why did we migrate? slide listing operating cost, maintenance and scalability reasons, Netcetera logo bottom left](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/netcetera-why-did-we-migrate.jpg)
*400k EUR a year in operating cost and scaling measured in days: the business case on one slide.*

Later slides walked through detailed architecture diagrams of the setup.

#### How the AI QA framework works

The `ai-qa-framework` session was a live demo rather than a slide talk, and my clips caught most of it. The speaker, who wasn't named on screen, started with the idea: point it at a URL and give it a few settings, such as how deep to go and which kinds of testing to run. Then the LLM takes over. It discovers the site by scraping, makes a plan, moves from the plan into execution, gathers the results and analyses them with the evidence, and presents them to the user. He said they had open-sourced it and the audience was welcome to use it.

![The ai-qa-framework GitHub repository on the big screen at Tolhuistuin, with the presenter holding a microphone beside it and the tarmac roll-up banner on the left](/blog/events/platform-engineering-meetup-amsterdam-human-intelligence-2026/ai-qa-framework-repo-walkthrough.jpg)
*The `ai-qa-framework` repository on screen during the walkthrough.*

He ran it from a terminal with `python -m src.cli run` against Sauce Demo, a public Shopify demo store. The log showed three stages:

1. **Crawl.** He had set it to look at 10 pages. The crawler spidered through the store: the home page, About Us, News, account registration and login, search, the product list and two jacket product pages. The log ended with "10 pages visited, 35 total URLs seen", in under half a minute. According to him, it also looks at previous runs, so it doesn't keep walking a well-worn path and leave other sections undiscovered. On screen, this showed up as a gap analysis before planning: 1 untested, 4 stale, 0 low-coverage and 3 failures.
2. **Plan.** The crawl result becomes a structured document with all the links it found. That goes to the LLM together with the kinds of testing wanted (he named functional, visual and security testing) and hints about the areas to cover, all loaded into the context. In the log, the call went to `claude-opus-4-6` with a 32,000-token limit. The answer took 94.6 seconds and came back as a plan of 19 test cases, with one validation warning (a "fill" step that had no value). The plan is then translated directly into Playwright actions.
3. **Execute.** He said the tests run in parallel on four threads to keep the run reasonably fast. The first tests on screen were functional ones: registration with valid data, login with invalid credentials, a password-reset form and adding a product to the cart from a product page. A security test for XSS injection in the search field came next. Adding to the cart passed. The login and registration tests failed and were re-run with video capture for failure analysis.

The part I found most practical was how it deals with flaky tests. As a developer, he said, one of the things he hates most is a fragile automation test, because he wants results back rather than time spent debugging tests that fail for no real reason. So there is a fallback. When a test fails, the framework takes a screenshot of the failing part and passes it back to the LLM with the plan, and the LLM decides whether the test should pass or fail. In the log, these were the "Calling AI with image" calls, capped at 500 tokens each and answered in about four to five seconds.

He also explained a design choice. For quality control you want deterministic flows that cover the core paths every time, but you also want some non-determinism in there. That's why they separated the two: rather than giving the LLM total control, they give it hints about what must be covered on every run. According to him, that's also why they didn't use MCP. He believed Playwright now has a tool of its own for this, which he thought would be interesting to add.

The [repository's README](https://github.com/brentkastner/ai-qa-framework) describes the same pipeline in four stages, adding a report stage that produces HTML and JSON reports with screenshots. According to the README, it tracks coverage between runs to target gaps, takes natural-language hints in a `qa-config.json` file, and can use a local Ollama model instead of the Anthropic API. It describes the screenshot step more broadly than the demo did, as AI-assisted error recovery that also fixes broken selectors.

My take: separating the paths that must be covered on every run from the parts the LLM can explore is the right call. I would still keep a small hand-written smoke suite for the critical paths, though. And when the LLM rules that a failed step is really a pass, I'd want a person to review that verdict before it counts as green in a CI gate.

## The community is the point

What I appreciate most about Platform Engineering Amsterdam is the quality of conversations that happen between and after the talks. The meetup has grown into one of the strongest PE communities in Europe, with 4.8 stars across 147 reviews — and it shows in the caliber of attendees.

Other communities got the mic too. Someone from the **MLOps Community** in Amsterdam, a local chapter of a global community, gave a short pitch. According to him, it has about 2,500 people on its Luma list, and its events range from 50 to 250 people: workshops, hackathons and meetups like this one. The community existed before the generative AI boom and still covers classical ML, but it now covers GenAI heavily too, including the technology behind coding assistants and how to build AI systems that are stable and scalable. Coming up, he announced an OpenClaw hackathon, plus "AI for education" and "AI for defence" specials later in the year.

The "Human Intelligence" theme was more than a catchy title. In a field obsessed with automation, the reminder that platforms are built by people, for people, was exactly right. The best platforms encode human judgment into reusable patterns — they do not replace it.
