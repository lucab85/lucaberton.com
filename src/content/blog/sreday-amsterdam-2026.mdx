![The opening of SREday Amsterdam 2026 on the main stage, the sponsors and partners on the screen](/blog/events/sreday-amsterdam-2026/sreday-2026-opening.jpg)

On Monday 2 November I was at **SREday Amsterdam 2026**, in ING Cedar on the Bijlmerdreef: one day, from 08:00 to 18:30, four tracks in four event halls. I came as a speaker, with a talk on the main stage in the afternoon, and with my microphones for [Udienza](/blog/udienza-video-podcast-launch-2026/), my video podcast. I also kept a recorder running in the rooms I sat in: fourteen recordings, about four and a half hours. This is the day from those recordings, the programme, the slides and the five conversations I recorded for the show.

## The welcome: SRE is invisible

ING was the host, and ING opened the day. The welcome started from two things that had just happened in the room: a go/no-go before the start, and someone who sat on a broken chair in the first row and then left it so that the next person could see it was broken. "That's closing the feedback loop", and that is SRE. Done well, nobody notices it: "SRE is invisible, because the output of outstanding SRE is outstanding systems".

The talk described three waves. The first, still under way, turns the logic of tech around from features first to production first: "What counts is not a feature. What counts is the quality of production." The comparison was with the car industry and *The Machine That Changed the World*: the engineer bolting a wheel onto a Toyota tells the designer that a different bolt would save ten seconds on a tool change, and the design changes. "SRE is an industrial practice."

The second wave is patching. ING has "multiplied by 6, roughly the amount of patches we are deploying", and the backlog stays about the same, because the count is not what it seems: "They are vulnerabilities times assets." What was good enough yesterday is not any more: "The good enough is no longer enough." The third wave is agentic engineering, with an open question for this room: what is an SLO for an agent? Cut its tokens when it breaks production, or build SRE agents whose job is to feed context back to the other agents?

The questions went further. On sovereignty, after Starlink was switched off in Ukraine, the question is no longer only where the data is but "Who can shut off my system?" On team shape: a separate SRE team, with the authority over the pipeline to say no, because squads under deadline pressure will compromise. On chaos engineering: worth it, though more interesting is the mutation testing a small team at ING is experimenting with, which shows how much of an artificially introduced bug the delivery process catches.

I asked one of the questions myself. I had been at the Agentic AI Foundation's event in San Francisco two weeks before, where the pressure that agent workloads put on infrastructure was everywhere, and I wanted to know where that leaves the cost of tokens. The answer: "it's not a matter of how much token we are spending, it's a matter of can we track the benefits". And code that agents generate has to be maintained, like any other code: about 6% of the engineering spent on new code comes back every year as run cost, and the same will happen with tokens. If every tribe lets its own agents invent their own architecture, "In three years we are there with a big shovel."

## The keynotes: who watches the agents?

The main stage then had four keynotes. Peter Marshall of Imply opened with *Agentic observability needs a new engine*. Lewis Isaac of Coralogix asked it outright: *Who's Watching the Agents? Measuring the Impact of AI-Assisted Development*. Stephen Cihak of Harness went one step further, to the agent that fixes what it finds: *Closing the Loop: From Production Incident to Autonomous Fix*. And Miko Pawlikowski, who co-founded SREday, brought *Ten years of Kubernetes lessons, in one binary*.

![Peter Marshall on the main stage for the first keynote, an Imply slide behind](/blog/events/sreday-amsterdam-2026/sreday-2026-imply-keynote.jpg)

Peter's keynote was an experiment. Two Imply customers had tried to build an AI agent on their log data, on a back end that was well tuned and on a fixed budget. To see what happens, "My team built this with Claude in three days": an agent working through a playbook of about 17 standard log searches, and a small Streamlit app to judge each investigation it carried out. The agent's reasoning was sensible: enrich the alert, ask whether it is normal, pivot to other data, work out the blast radius. What it did to the back end was not. A five-minute detection window became, at the median, a search over 800 hours of logs. "So one query turned into 26." A search on four fields turned into 15, three of them not indexed, so it ran regular expressions over the raw data. The most extreme investigation scanned 1.4 million rows.

His conclusions: an agent is obsessed with the goal it is given, so when it cannot get the data it will make something up, and when it hits a timeout it does not shorten the query unless told to. Detection is tractable; analysis handed to an agent has a load on disks, CPUs and memory that is "extremely unpredictable". Agents also want old data immediately, while "historical data is treated as a second-class citizen in these platforms". Imply's answer, in four steps: decouple storage from compute, store and compress logs differently, make the compute elastic so that the agents run apart from the main monitoring workload, and federate. "These are not mind-blowing ideas to anybody who lives through the analytics revolution." Asked whether Imply Lumi is software as a service or self-hosted: both, because regulated industries, banks, governments and telecoms, need it on their own infrastructure.

## Harness engineering, from Rabobank

Before lunch, an engineer from Rabobank talked about harness engineering: the harness is what sits between the model and your code, as a harness once stopped a horse and cart. Two examples started it. Ask an agent to make a red test green, and it may do it by changing the assertion, because the goal was a green test. Add a parameter to a discount function, and the change ripples through every caller, a domino effect the agent misses when the context window moves on.

A harness, in this talk, has five parts: context (the MD files, skills and specs), tooling, looping (give the agent a goal and loop until the tests pass), verification, and guardrails with sandboxing. Rules written in an agent's MD file are not deterministic, so the advice was: "Try to make them as hooks, not a text in your MD file." Then CI gates of your own on top, for example one that refuses a commit changing a test and its implementation together. "AI agent is not your partner." Give it a concrete plan. The second half was a code graph: the speaker built one of the repository with Graphify, stored in JSON or Neo4j and refreshed by hooks on every commit, so that an agent can ask what a refactor will touch, and the tokens per question went down ten times.

## OpenTelemetry for the agents

Another talk asked the question every vendor demo skips: "would you let an agent act on production?" A hotel's chat agent, asked about the other of two locations, first said there was no other location, then agreed there was; that is not the conversation you want at 3 a.m. on call. Vendor demos stop at the fix: "the demo usually ends at the fix", and nobody shows the reasoning.

The answer was OpenTelemetry, in three parts. The instrumentation, which gives you traces, metrics and logs out of the box. The semantic conventions, the shared names for methods, status codes and units that an agent needs to reason the same way about every service. And the collector, because "Observability is about cutting through the noise and finding and retaining only the signals that actually matter." What is still in the on-call engineer's head goes into knowledge bases and skills, and the GenAI attributes let you trace whether the agent actually used them. The agent, too, has to "earn the keys" to production. From the audience came a question about Weaver, the OpenTelemetry sub-project for your own conventions: useful, but static, and the asker wanted those semantics carried through the pipeline with the data.

## The afternoon in event hall 2

After lunch I sat in event hall 2. Thijs Feryn of Varnish Software went first, with three statements he has asked audiences for years: "do we agree that slow websites suck?", that performance is part of the user experience, and that a slow website is as bad as one that is down. Throwing servers at it no longer works: "more servers, more problems". Caching does, and in 2026 the surprise is what gets cached: "our main source of acceleration is now software packages", Docker images, npm and Maven packages.

Then the news. "Varnish 9 is by far the most feature-rich addition to Varnish", and its first contribution is native TLS, so that after twenty years Varnish no longer needs a TLS proxy in front of it, with certificates loaded and reloaded without restarting. Dynamic backends resolve DNS at request time instead of at compile time, through a Rust module, part of a Rust framework for writing Varnish modules without C. There are a Helm chart and an officially supported Gateway API for Kubernetes, which applies no caching until you define a policy. And for those who would rather not run it, a CDN: "It's fully sovereign, we're a European company."

Piotr Zaniewski of vCluster Labs followed, and his title meant exactly what it said: he curled a GPU into his cluster. vCluster started from "We like Kubernetes so much that we could create Kubernetes inside of Kubernetes." On stage, the control plane and one worker lived on his laptop, in Docker containers, and a change to a containerised web app was rebuilt and running again in four seconds. The GPU was a Google Cloud VM with an NVIDIA T4, which got its driver, Ollama and a model at first boot. Then: "We are curling the GPU into our cluster because the join command is simply a curl." The private-nodes slide listed what any host needs to join, whether a KVM VM, a cloud VM or bare metal: one curl command, an expiring join token, and a VPN built on the Tailscale SDK and WireGuard, with no Tailscale account. The join can be fully automated, and the GPU node goes away when the developer is done with it.

The demo app was CLASSIF, Piotr's open-source classifier, served from that GPU by a small decision model that answers with one label and a probability instead of paragraphs. Asked whether a broken coffee machine on floor 2 was a Kubernetes problem, it said no, with 99%.

## When everyone has an agent

Late in the afternoon came a talk about the people outside IT, the normies, as the speaker called them, and "what a normie would do if they had a harness": nightly briefs from news feeds, analysis, training material, and expenses. Even one person's slides cost tokens: the deck of that talk took about $2,500 of them. Multiply by thousands of employees, each building their own harness, and the platform problem comes back. Developers have learnt the SDLC; "it turns out the AI also doesn't really care about SDLC. It cares about getting a job done." So the platform has to give agents one paved way: consuming services declaratively instead of provisioning them, and control points such as gateways. The example: at Broadcom, every developer's MCP access, to GitHub, Jira and the rest, goes through one MCP gateway. And with agents that fetch whatever they need from the internet, the supply chain becomes everybody's problem.

The last talk I recorded was ClickHouse's, on memory for an SRE agent. The scenario: a P99 latency spike, with red herrings around it, caused by a new deployment that "cuts the database connection pool from 50 to 5". There were six scenarios, easy, hard and hidden, over two days: on the second day a similar spike with a new cause, and a local memory in chDB, which the agent reaches through a tool called recall findings, to remember what happened the day before. With or without memory, the strongest models found the easy cases, and "The model is taking 17 seconds to return the answer", while the memory lookup adds milliseconds. The case for memory grows with time and with a whole team sharing it. One practical note for anyone who tries it with the session files of their coding agents: "They may contain sensitive data."

## My talk: The GPU Is Not Your SLO

At 16:30 it was my turn, on the main stage, with *The GPU Is Not Your SLO: Reliability Engineering for Production AI*: a post-mortem of a Monday morning on a small H100 cluster, a composite of several incidents, from 8:55 to 10:30. Kubernetes with the GPU operator, vLLM serving a chat assistant, Prometheus and Grafana, and three tenants sharing the GPUs by time slicing: the chat, a nightly batch job and the notebooks.

At 8:55 the dashboard was green, with 94% GPU utilization, "And every one of these numbers is true." Then the tickets came, and a Slack thread with 40 people in it. Time to first token and the latency between tokens had both gone up. Inside vLLM, 140 requests were waiting and the KV cache was at 98%. The cause: the nightly batch, meant for the weekend, had been started at 9:02 and ran for 36 minutes, on a cluster with no priority class and no quota. So "the dashboard was not lying", it just did not show the impact on the users. Scaling the batch to zero fixed it; shortening the timeout from 30 seconds to 10, which we tried along the way, made it worse, because every slow request became a timeout plus a retry. Then finance asked what the incident cost.

The lessons: "nobody measured the user experience", and now we do, on a dashboard with the queue time, the saturation, the time to first token and between tokens, the usable answers and the cost. SLOs agreed with the product owner. Clear ownership of each part of the platform, and a shared error budget. Priorities and queues for the GPUs, so that the batch waits for the chat, a limit on the backlog, MIG slices instead of time slicing where the isolation is worth it, and a benchmark of every endpoint to find its breaking point before the users do.

## In the hallways

Between talks, the stands. At ClickHouse, the talk went on: you can store vectors in ClickHouse and compare them with a cosine distance in plain SQL, so the memory of an agent can be searched semantically instead of being read as an ever longer text. At Netdata, the idea is to put the knowledge about metrics into the tool, so that nobody has to be a part-time statistician to build a dashboard at 3 a.m. Their AI troubleshooting already has a story: a customer's team had spent three days on a root cause analysis, and the same question to the tool came back with the same answer in a few minutes.
