## 1. Why TNW still matters

**TNW Conference** bills itself as *“the most talked‑about tech festival in Europe.”* After two sun‑splashed days on Amsterdam’s NDSM wharf, I get why: it’s equal parts business conference and music‑festival energy. With **4,500+ tech enthusiasts**, **2,000+ startups**, and **675+ investors** roaming repurposed shipyards, the collision density for serendipity is off the charts.

## 2. Festival vibes & first impressions

* Open‑air venue ringed by shipping containers → instant street‑art aesthetic.
* Lanyards color‑coded by persona (startup, investor, corp) → networking on easy‑mode.
* Coffee trucks, silent‑disco headsets, and a DJ booth next to the main stage remind you this *isn’t* your average expo hall.

### Hugging Face on open source, start-ups and robots

I only recorded the last part of the open-source session, so this is partial: the question-and-answer stretch with the Hugging Face co-founder named on the slide, and it is what I could follow from the audio.

**Why founders should think twice about the middle layer.** Asked where entrepreneurs should aim, he described three layers. The bottom is chips and low-level platform work, which he finds interesting: his argument was that the price of AI compute will converge towards the price of electricity, so every electron should go into useful AI computation, which GPUs today are not. He mentioned the Netherlands' strength in this layer through ASML. The middle layer, training foundation models from scratch, he called not very exciting for entrepreneurs right now: very capital-intensive, hard to hire for and competitive worldwide. The top layer, the application layer, is where he sees most recent success stories, many of them European. Most users, he said, want AI inside the tools and workflows they already use, not just a chatbot, and people switch between open and closed models often. His practical advice was to take an old field that has not changed in ten years, such as supply chain, compliance or accounting, and rebuild it with an AI-first approach. He also made a point that very large compensation packages at big tech can pull founders away from starting companies, which he said is bad for the ecosystem.

**Robots.** In the last clip he explained how Hugging Face moved into robotics. The first step was making cheap robots accessible: a very simple open-source robot arm at about 100 dollars, which people used to start small teams and start-ups around tasks like putting things in packages or folding. A couple of months earlier the company had acquired Pollen Robotics, which he described as one of maybe two open-source hardware robotics companies in the world; he said that when Pollen was about to raise money, he suggested they join Hugging Face instead. The next stage is more robot types at accessible price points, so that people can build their own businesses on a basic platform, as they did in software.

My take: the "pick the app layer and rebuild an old industry" advice is what I tell clients about AI products too, with one addition: the app layer is only defensible if your data and workflow integration are hard to copy, which is why it pairs well with the open-model choice he described.

> 💡 **Hallway takeaway:** Investors asked less about TAM, more about *path to profitability & AI defensibility*.
